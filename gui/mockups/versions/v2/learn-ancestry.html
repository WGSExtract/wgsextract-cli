Ancestry & relatives

Haplogroups follow two single edges of your family tree, while autosomal models and kinship compare shuffled DNA across hundreds of ancestors.

Y-DNA and mtDNA follow two unbroken lines

A haplogroup names a migration-tree branch defined by shared mutations. Y-chromosome DNA (Y-DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid) passes father to son, so Reginald carries Gordon’s J-ZS241. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) passes from mother to child through the egg: Reginald shares Helen’s K1a1b1a, while Gordon’s H1c stops with Gordon and Helen would need a male paternal relative’s file to read her paternal line.

Ten generations back you have 1,024 ancestor slots; Y-DNA and mtDNA trace just two of them. Surname projects also compare faster-mutating Y-chromosome short tandem repeats (Y-STRs), though short reads size long repeats less cleanly than dedicated panels, and cells can carry a benign mtDNA mixture called heteroplasmy.

How autosomal ancestry percentages are modeled

Ancestry proportions compare shuffled markers across chromosomes 1–22 against reference populations from cohorts like the Human Genome Diversity Project (HGDP) and 1000 Genomes (2,504–3,202 donors).

Reginald’s 52% European / 38% Middle Eastern result shows which blend of modern reference samples best fits his markers; neighboring groups grade into each other and changing the reference panel shifts the numbers.

Checking family ties across files on your disk

Relatedness and kinship tools measure shared DNA between files in your library, confirming Gordon and Reginald share ~50% across every autosome or catching sample swaps, while 25%-sharing ties (grandparent, aunt/uncle, half-sibling) need family context to separate. Local checks compare only loaded files; uploading an export to an outside matching database is a separate choice that can uncover unexpected relatives.

What uncertainty bars capture and what they miss

A 95% interval shows block-resampling wobble inside one model without capturing ancestral groups missing from the reference panel altogether. Pair estimates with family records; statistical similarity never defines culture or belonging.
