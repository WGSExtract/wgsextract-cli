Ancestry & relatives

A direct lineage, a population comparison and a family relationship are different questions.

Haplogroups trace one line at a time

A haplogroup is a named branch defined by shared variants in a lineage tree. Y-DNA traces the largely non-recombining Y chromosome along a direct paternal line. mtDNA traces a direct maternal line and is usually inherited through the egg.

One line is only one of many ancestors each generation. Neither haplogroup describes your overall ancestry or nationality. Y analysis needs usable Y sequence; a relative on the relevant paternal line may help when your own data has none.

Y-STRs are repeat counts compared by surname projects. They can help distinguish paternal lines, but mutations and measurement differences complicate matches. A haplogroup match alone does not establish a recent relationship.

mtDNA can contain a mixture of sequence versions, called heteroplasmy. Levels can differ between tissues and relatives; they are not automatically a sign of disease.

Autosomal ancestry is a comparison model

Ancestry proportions compare patterns across chromosomes 1–22 with reference populations. Collections such as HGDP and 1000 Genomes contain sampled people, not a complete catalogue of ancestral groups.

A component labelled after a place means resemblance to those samples under that model. It does not mean exactly that share of your recent ancestors held that nationality. Models can split or combine related groups differently.

Relatedness compares shared inherited DNA

Relatedness and kinship estimates help identify close relatives, duplicate samples and possible mix-ups. Parent–child and full siblings share similar overall amounts but different patterns; some relationship categories overlap statistically.

A local comparison only compares the files you provide. It does not search a company's match database. Uploading to a matching service is a separate decision and can reveal unexpected family relationships.

Uncertainty is more than an interval

A 95% interval summarizes uncertainty under a stated statistical method. It does not necessarily capture errors caused by an unrepresentative reference panel, missing markers or a poor model.

More measured markers can improve precision, but not fix every bias. Keep family records and context alongside genetic evidence; ancestry estimates do not define culture or identity.
