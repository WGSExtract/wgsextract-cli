Ancestry & relatives

Haplogroups trace two single lines of your family tree; autosomal models and kinship compare DNA shuffled across hundreds of ancestors.

Y-DNA and mtDNA haplogroups

A haplogroup names a migration-tree branch defined by shared mutations. Y-chromosome DNA (Y-DNA) passes from father to son, so Reginald carries Gordon’s J-ZS241; Helen has no Y chromosome and would need a file from a male relative on her father’s side to read that line. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) passes from mother to child through the egg, so Reginald carries Helen’s K1a1b1a and Gordon’s H1c stops with Gordon.

Ten generations back you have 1,024 ancestor slots, and Y-DNA and mtDNA trace just two of them. Surname projects also compare faster-mutating Y-chromosome short tandem repeats (Y-STRs), where short reads size long repeats less cleanly than dedicated panels. In mitochondria, cells can also carry a benign mixture of mtDNA sequences called heteroplasmy.

Autosomal ancestry percentages

Ancestry proportions compare shuffled markers across chromosomes 1–22 against reference populations from cohorts like the Human Genome Diversity Project (HGDP) and 1000 Genomes (2,504–3,202 donors).

Reginald’s 52% European / 38% Middle Eastern result shows which blend of modern reference samples best fits his markers; neighboring groups grade into each other and changing the reference panel shifts the numbers.

Checking family ties across files on your disk

Relatedness and kinship tools measure shared DNA between files in your library, confirming that Gordon and Reginald share ~50% across every autosome or catching an accidental sample swap. Relationships that share ~25% on average (grandparent, aunt or uncle, half-sibling) still need family context to separate. Local checks compare only loaded files; uploading an export to an outside matching database is a separate choice that can uncover unexpected relatives.

Confidence ranges and reference gaps

A 95% interval measures how much a percentage shifts when chromosome blocks are resampled inside one model. It cannot flag ancestral groups missing from the reference panel altogether. Because these percentages measure marker similarity to modern donor cohorts, family records remain essential for tracing specific places and communities.
