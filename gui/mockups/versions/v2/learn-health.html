Interpreting health findings

How software compares your variants against clinical records, population frequencies, effect predictors and drug-response genes.

Before acting on anything health-related

WGS Extract runs research tools on personal files; its outputs are not clinical diagnoses and cannot rule a condition out. Do not change medications or screening based on results here. Bring any concerning finding to a physician or genetic counsellor, who can arrange accredited clinical testing.

How annotation labels a raw variant

Annotation tools such as Ensembl’s Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) compare each variant in a VCF (Variant Call Format) file against known gene transcripts, estimating how the DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) change affects RNA (ribonucleic acid) or protein sequence and attaching population frequencies and clinical records. A missense variant swaps one amino acid in a protein; many are benign, and the same variant can receive different labels on alternate transcripts of one gene.

Reading ClinVar entries and review stars

ClinVar (Clinical Variation database) gathers lab interpretations scored zero to four stars by reviewer consensus. Check the disorder, stars, dates and inheritance together: one recessive copy (0/1) ordinarily marks unaffected carrier status, and many dominant variants show incomplete penetrance, meaning not everyone with the variant develops the condition.

Sparse or divided evidence earns a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) label, which guidelines treat as unactionable alone. Reports also cite the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) in two ways: the ACMG Secondary Findings list of actionable genes, and the joint ACMG and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) evidence rules labs use to classify variants.

Checking how common a variant is in gnomAD

The Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) reports allele frequency (AF) across hundreds of thousands of people, counting chromosome copies (AF 0.01 = 1% of measured copies). High frequency rules out a solo cause of a rare Mendelian disorder, whereas absence from gnomAD is ordinary for benign private variants or under-sampled ancestries.

Computational scores rank candidates

  • Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores genome-wide changes; a scaled score of 20 sits in the top 1% of predicted-damaging substitutions, without stating a probability of disease.
  • Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL) scores missense changes from 0 to 1; protein-structure predictors such as AlphaMissense use the same 0-to-1 scale.
  • SpliceAI, a deep-learning model from Illumina, scores from 0 to 1 how likely a variant is to change how RNA is spliced.

Because many predictors draw on the same evolutionary conservation data, matching high scores often reflect one shared signal and routinely flag benign variants.

Genes that influence how medicines work

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) studies genes affecting drug metabolism; the Pharmacogenomics Clinical Annotation Tool (PharmCAT) links genotypes to Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guidance using named star alleles like CYP2C19*2.

Unread sites in a variant-only file like Helen’s VCF leave star-allele blind spots, and structurally complex genes such as CYP2D6 (cytochrome P450 2D6) and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) loci require specialized callers and clinical typing. Even an accurate genotype is only one piece of drug response, which also depends on organ function and interacting drugs.

Polygenic scores and population percentiles

A polygenic score (PGS), called a polygenic risk score (PRS) when applied to disease, sums small weights across thousands of common alleles. Landing in the 80th percentile means scoring higher than 80% of a comparison group, not having an 80% chance of illness; actual risk depends on baseline prevalence, clinical factors and how closely your ancestry matches the study cohort.
