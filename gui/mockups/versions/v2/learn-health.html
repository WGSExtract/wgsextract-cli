Interpreting health findings

A personal report describes software findings. It is not a diagnosis or medical advice.

Pause before acting on a result

Do not change treatment or screening based on this exploration. Bring concerning findings to a qualified clinician or genetic counsellor, who can assess your history and arrange clinical confirmation. A “nothing found” report does not rule out a genetic condition.

Annotation adds context, not certainty

Annotation attaches gene names, predicted consequences, frequencies and published interpretations to a VCF. VEP, the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor, predicts consequences relative to gene transcripts.

A missense change alters a predicted protein building block; it does not automatically damage the protein. Results depend on transcript, resource version and build. Annotation cannot fix a false genotype.

ClinVar labels need a condition and evidence

ClinVar collects submitted clinical interpretations. Check the condition, review status, date and disagreements. “Pathogenic” means disease-causing in an appropriate context, not “this person is ill.”

Inheritance matters: one allele for a recessive condition often means carrier status, while risk for other conditions depends on age, penetrance and other factors. A VUS, variant of uncertain significance, is not a positive diagnosis and should not alone direct care.

ACMG means American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics. Its secondary-findings gene list defines a scope for clinical evaluation; it is not a list where every variant is harmful. The classification framework co-published with AMP, the Association for Molecular Pathology, is a separate evidence process, not a simple score threshold.

Rare does not mean harmful

gnomAD, the Genome Aggregation Database, reports variant observations across research cohorts. Allele frequency counts allele copies, not people.

A variant too common for a proposed rare disorder is evidence against that interpretation. But a variant absent from gnomAD is not automatically harmful: it may be rare, poorly covered or from a population under-represented in the dataset. Participants are not a uniformly healthy control group.

Prediction scores help prioritize — not diagnose

  • CADD ranks predicted deleteriousness; a scaled score of 20 is roughly a top-1% rank among possible substitutions, not a 99% disease probability.
  • REVEL combines models for missense variants. AlphaMissense is another missense predictor.
  • SpliceAI predicts changes to RNA splicing, the cutting and joining of a gene’s RNA.

Models can share training evidence, disagree and be wrong. Several high scores are not automatically several independent lines of clinical evidence.

Pharmacogenomics is not a prescription

Pharmacogenomics studies genetic effects on medicine response. PharmCAT translates supported genotypes into allele calls and published guideline annotations.

A star allele is a named gene version such as CYP2C19*2. Missing defining sites cannot safely be assumed reference. Complex genes such as CYP2D6 and highly variable HLA genes need specialist analysis.

Other medicines, kidney and liver function, age and diagnosis also affect prescribing. Review clinically validated findings with a clinician or pharmacist; never change a dose yourself from this report.

A percentile is not your chance of disease

A PGS, polygenic score, sums many weighted alleles. A disease-oriented score is often called a PRS, polygenic risk score.

“80th percentile” means higher than 80% of a specified comparison group, not an 80% disease risk. Scores need appropriate ancestry representation and calibration; environment and clinical factors still matter. They neither guarantee nor exclude a future outcome.
