DNA & genomes

Your cells carry a 3.1-billion-letter text inherited from your parents, and a genome file is a lab’s working readout of it.

Four letters arranged in pairs

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) stores inherited instructions using four chemical bases: A, C, G and T. Two chains twist together like a spiral ladder where A pairs across the rung with T and C pairs with G along the opposite strand.

Each paired rung is one base pair (bp), so a 150 bp read covers 150 ladder positions rather than 300 independent letters.

How chromosomes divide up the genome

Nuclear DNA is packaged into chromosomes. Most cells hold two copies of chromosomes 1–22 (autosomes), one from each parent, plus sex chromosomes; XX and XY are the most common pairings, though other complements and mosaic cell mixtures occur naturally.

In the library, Reginald McDonald inherited half his autosomes and his Y chromosome from his father Gordon, and the other half plus his X chromosome from his mother Helen. Outside the nucleus, cell mitochondria carry a separate 16,569-base ring of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) passed from Helen to Reginald through the egg cell.

One set of nuclear chromosomes spans roughly 3.1 billion bases. A whole-genome test reads across nearly all of them, leaving gaps mainly inside dense repeats.

Protein-coding genes take up under 2% of your DNA

A gene is a stretch of DNA copied into RNA (ribonucleic acid), often as the template for building a protein. Protein-coding segments make up under 2% of human DNA.

Much of the remaining 98% regulates when and where genes switch on alongside long repeat stretches. Many changes inside genes are harmless, while certain regulatory changes outside genes still matter.

What a variant actually describes

Unrelated people match at about 99.9% of bases, leaving 4 to 5 million small differences per genome. Software spots them by comparing your reads against a reference genome, a shared coordinate map built from volunteer donors.

Carrying a G where that map has an A is logged as a variant; the alternative versions at one site are alleles. Almost all of those millions of variants are ordinary human diversity, and naming any variant requires its reference build, chromosome, position and letter change.
