How sequencing works

Many overlapping observations become an estimate of your DNA sequence.

From sample to digital letters

A lab extracts DNA from a sample such as blood or saliva, prepares molecules for a sequencer, and records signals from them. Software called a basecaller turns those signals into estimated A, C, G and T letters with quality scores. The lab commonly delivers these as FASTQ files.

This differs from a microarray, the physical chip used by many ancestry tests. An array tests selected known positions; sequencing reads stretches of DNA and can find differences the chip was not designed to test.

A read is one observation of a fragment

A read is a string of estimated DNA letters. A short-read genome often has hundreds of millions of reads. They overlap different parts of the genome, like many snippets from a long book.

Paired-end sequencing reads both ends of a DNA fragment. “2 × 150 bp” means two linked 150-base reads, usually in R1 and R2 files. It does not mean the whole fragment between them was read.

Short and long reads answer different questions

  • Illumina commonly produces accurate short reads, often 100–150 bases. Repeated DNA can make their placement ambiguous.
  • PacBio HiFi repeatedly reads circularized templates to produce accurate long reads, often around 10–25 kb.
  • Oxford Nanopore (ONT) measures electrical signals as molecules pass through pores. Reads can be tens of kb or longer; chemistry and basecalling models affect accuracy.

Long reads can bridge repeats, larger changes and distant variants. Read N50 summarizes length by bases, not accuracy. Use aligner presets and caller models matching the technology.

Suitable long-read data can also preserve evidence of DNA methylation, chemical marks that do not change the base sequence. Ordinary Illumina whole-genome reads do not measure those marks.

30× is an average, not a guarantee

Depth counts reads covering a position. A mean of 30× means roughly 30 observations per base across the chosen region. Some positions may have none, others many more; duplicate or ambiguously mapped reads may not be usable.

“Coverage” can also mean the fraction of bases reaching a threshold, such as 95% at 10× or more. Check which definition a chart uses. One-copy regions commonly have less depth than two-copy autosomes.

Three quality questions — three different scores

  • Base quality: is this letter correct? Q30 corresponds to a modeled 1-in-1,000 error probability.
  • MAPQ, mapping quality: is the read placed in the right region?
  • GQ, genotype quality: how confident is a caller in the genotype inferred from the reads?

These are software estimates. High depth does not erase mapping bias or contamination. Unmapped reads may be low quality, missing human sequence or other organisms; they do not prove infection.
