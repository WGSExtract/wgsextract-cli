How sequencing works

Sequencers cannot read a whole chromosome in one piece, so labs read hundreds of millions of overlapping snippets instead.

Saliva or blood in, letter files out

Labs extract DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) from saliva or blood, break it into fragments and run those molecules past optical or electrical sensors. Basecalling software converts the raw signals into A, C, G and T strings plus quality scores in FASTQ (FASTA with Quality scores) files.

Consumer kits like Alex’s 23andMe file use a microarray chip that probes ~640,000 fixed markers. Whole-genome sequencing reads continuous fragments across every chromosome, catching rare variants that chips never test.

Reading both ends of each DNA fragment

Each sequenced snippet is a read; Reginald’s genome holds 876 million of them, stacked so random read errors wash out.

Most short-read runs are paired-end (“2 × 150 bp”, meaning two reads of 150 base pairs): from a fragment several hundred bases long, the machine reads 150 bases from one end into R1 and 150 from the other into R2, using the known spacing between mates to guide alignment.

Short reads and long reads

  • Illumina produces accurate 100–150 bp short reads at low cost, though short pieces map ambiguously inside long repeats.
  • PacBio HiFi (Pacific Biosciences High-Fidelity) reads circularized fragments repeatedly to yield accurate 10–25 kilobase reads.
  • Oxford Nanopore (ONT) decodes electrical current shifts across tiny pores for reads tens of kilobases or longer, with accuracy tied to chemistry and basecaller version.

Long reads span repeats and structural rearrangements directly. Labs summarize piece size with read N50 (half of all sequenced bases sit in reads at least N50 long) and match callers to each platform’s error profile. When modification tags are kept, PacBio and Nanopore runs also retain native DNA methylation marks that standard Illumina amplification erases.

What 30× depth looks like up close

Depth counts usable reads overlapping one base. A “30×” run averages 30 reads per site, while local depth waves from 25–40× in easy stretches down to zero in stubborn repeats after duplicates and multi-mapped reads are set aside.

“Coverage” can mean average depth (38.2× on Gordon’s file) or the share of bases clearing a bar like 10×. Copy count shows right in those numbers: Reginald’s paired autosomes average ~31× while his single X and Y sit near 14–16×.

Quality scores from single letters up to genotypes

  • Base quality uses the logarithmic Phred scale per read letter: Q20 estimates a 1-in-100 error rate and Q30 estimates 1 in 1,000.
  • Mapping quality (MAPQ) scores confidence in where the whole read landed, dropping near zero inside duplicated sequence.
  • Genotype quality (GQ) scores how strongly stacked reads favor the called genotype over the runner-up.

Systematic mapping bias or sample contamination from another person’s DNA can fool those math models. Roughly 1% of reads also stay unmapped, usually noisy tails, reference gaps or harmless mouth bacteria from saliva.
