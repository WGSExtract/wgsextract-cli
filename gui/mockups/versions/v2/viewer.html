How to read it

The coloured strip at the top is the reference sequence, one colour per base. Under each person’s name is a grey graph of depth, how many reads cover each base; a coloured bar in it marks a position where many reads disagree with the reference. Below that, each grey bar is one read, and a coloured mark on a read is a base that differs from the reference.

Scroll or drag to move, and use + and − to zoom. Click a read for its details. More in Reads and depth.

APOE: one copy of ε4

At rs429358 about half of Reginald’s reads show a C where the reference has T. Half means one copy: he is heterozygous. Together with the reference base at the neighbouring rs7412, that C makes an ε4 allele. Helen’s VCF has the same call, so this copy came from her.

One ε4 copy raises the average risk of late-onset Alzheimer’s disease. It doesn’t mean someone will get it. Read how risk variants are rated before deciding to look at results like this.

HFE C282Y: one copy

Reginald and Gordon each carry one A at rs1800562. Two copies cause most cases of hereditary haemochromatosis, where the body stores too much iron. One copy makes them carriers, which is common in people of northern European descent and usually harmless.

Compare the colours: the A is green in roughly half the reads, which is what one copy looks like.

MTHFR C677T

Turn on Alex: every one of her reads has the A, so she has two copies (homozygous). Reginald, Gordon and Helen have one. About one person in ten has two copies. Labs and supplement sellers make much of it, but current guidelines advise against testing for it.

BRCA1: a change that’s harmless

BRCA1 is a cancer gene, and seeing a variant in it can be alarming. This one, rs1799966, is listed as benign in ClinVar: about one person in three carries it. Being in an important gene says nothing on its own about whether a variant causes harm.

LCT: digesting milk as an adult

The A at rs4988235, upstream of the lactase gene in MCM6, keeps lactase switched on after childhood. Gordon has two copies; Reginald and Alex have one. One copy is usually enough to digest milk.

Y chromosome: M304

Every one of Reginald’s and Gordon’s reads has C at M304, a marker that defines the J haplogroup. The Y passes from father to son, so they match. Men have one Y, so there’s no half-and-half here, and depth is half that of the other chromosomes.

Helen and Alex have no Y chromosome, so their tracks are empty. Their lineage is read from mtDNA instead. See Reginald’s paternal line.

mtDNA: K1a1b1a or H1c

At 16224 and 16311 Reginald’s reads show C, two of the markers that place him in K. Turn on Gordon: no change at those spots. Gordon is H1c, and his mitochondria aren’t passed to his children. Reginald’s came from Helen, whose VCF has the same calls.

Depth is far higher here than elsewhere because each cell holds hundreds of copies of mtDNA. More in Lineage.

A variant neither parent has

Reginald has an A here in about half his reads. Gordon’s reads all show the reference G, and his depth is good, so he truly doesn’t have it. Helen’s VCF is blank, but a VCF only lists differences, so blank could mean “reference” or “not checked”. A de novo call needs both parents’ reads to be sure.

Everyone carries around 60 new variants. Most are harmless. The trio tool finds them.

A 4-base deletion

Some of Reginald’s reads have a black line where four bases (GGCT) are missing. Helen’s VCF has the same deletion, and Gordon’s reads don’t, so it came from her.

A 1,200-base deletion

Depth drops to about half across 1.2 kb in Reginald and Gordon. Reads that cross the edge are cut off where the missing stretch begins (soft-clipped, shown as coloured ends), and pairs that straddle it are coloured because they sit further apart than usual. One copy of chr20 is missing this stretch. A deletion this size doesn’t show up in a normal variant call; it needs a CNV or SV caller.

An artifact: it’s all on one strand

Reads are now coloured by direction, one per row: pink reads run forward, blue ones in reverse. The T appears only on reverse reads, with low base quality (faded letters). A real variant appears on reads of both directions. The variant caller skipped this one, which is right. See How sure a call is.

SMN1: reads that fit two places

SMN1 has a near-identical copy, SMN2, nearby. Reads here could belong to either, so the aligner gives them MAPQ 0 and they appear hollow. The scattered mismatches are SMN2 reads parked on SMN1. Variant callers ignore these reads, so ordinary calls here are unreliable. Use the copy-number tool for SMN instead.
