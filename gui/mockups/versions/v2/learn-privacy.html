Privacy & sharing

A genome file uniquely identifies you, and because each parent and child shares half of that DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), choices about your file affect close relatives too.

Analysis runs on the computer holding your files

WGS Extract processes BAM (Binary Alignment Map), CRAM (Compressed Reference-oriented Alignment Map) and VCF (Variant Call Format) files on the computer hosting its local server. Opening the app from a phone over Wi-Fi uses the phone only as a remote screen, whereas pointing the app at another server trusts that machine with your files.

This clickable mockup uses fictional genomes and simulated runs. Because its review bar saves notes to a shared database, never paste real genotypes or medical details into review notes.

Fetching public reference files versus sending sample data

Downloading public references, ClinVar releases or containers (such as the 0.9 GB hs38d1 reference for Alex’s BAM) makes a normal web connection from your IP (Internet Protocol) address and sends none of your sample DNA.

Default analyses never upload sample positions or genotypes; any remote lookup that sends sample data requires an explicit opt-in naming what goes where. External reference links open public sites in a new tab, and Learn links stay inside the app.

Sharing the smallest slice that answers the question

  • A single-gene region extract or filtered variant table exposes far less than a 90 GB BAM, even though any slice of personal DNA remains sensitive.
  • Stripping your name never anonymizes DNA: internal headers keep sample tags, and a few dozen markers identify people via relative databases.
  • Before uploading a kit to a matching site, read its rules on law-enforcement queries, research opt-ins, retention and backup deletion.
  • Store backups on encrypted drives, use access-controlled transfers and never post sample slices on public issue trackers.

Ask permission before analyzing or uploading a relative’s file. Reginald shares half his autosomal DNA and his Y-DNA with Gordon, and the other half plus his mtDNA with Helen, so uploading one person’s file reveals data about family members who never chose to test.
